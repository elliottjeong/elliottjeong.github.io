I Was Just Looking at Research Careers

Then this happened

I started by looking at a few very different research paths in robotics and asking GPT what made each of them work — or not work — as a career trajectory.

Some were more hardware-heavy, some more method-driven, some more academic, some more applied. The more I asked about those differences, the less the conversation became about any particular researcher.

It gradually landed on a much more useful question:

What actually makes someone a good researcher?

GPT gave me an answer that I found pretty useful, and I wanted to keep it somewhere I could come back to.

1. Solving problems is not enough. Learn to choose good problems.

Early on, research can feel like a sequence of assignments: here is a problem, now find a clever way to solve it. But stronger researchers seem to gradually move one level upward and start asking whether the problem itself is worth solving. Why does it matter? Why now? Why me? What changes if it is solved? Will anyone care beyond this particular project?

Technical ability determines whether I can solve a problem. Research taste determines whether the problem was worth solving in the first place. I need both. Being technically excellent at an unimportant problem may still produce impressive work, but it does not necessarily produce important research.

2. I want my projects to form a trajectory, not a pile.

A list of unrelated projects can demonstrate versatility, but a strong research career often reads differently. One question leads naturally to another, one method exposes a limitation that motivates the next project, and a small problem gradually expands into a broader research agenda.

The individual projects do not need to look identical. What matters is that there is some recognizable thread connecting them. Over time, I want to be able to answer: What question do I keep returning to, even when the projects change?

I do not think I need that answer immediately. Exploration is useful, especially early on. But eventually, I want my work to look less like a collection of things I happened to try and more like a direction I am deliberately developing.

3. Breadth should be a toolbox, not an identity crisis.

Robotics makes it very tempting to learn everything: mechanics, control, electronics, computer vision, machine learning, simulation, interaction, fabrication. I actually like that about robotics. It is one of the reasons the field is so interesting.

But breadth alone does not create a research identity. The point of having many tools is not to prove that I know many tools. It is to be able to reach for whichever one a good research question requires.

Breadth is the toolbox. The research question is the direction.

That distinction feels important. I want to keep expanding what I can do without confusing “I can do many things” with “I know what I am trying to understand.”

4. Being able to build real things is a serious research advantage.

There is enormous value in being able to make an actual robot work. A physical system has imperfect sensors, noisy actuators, calibration problems, latency, mechanical tolerances, unexpected interactions between components, and all the other ways reality refuses to behave like a clean simulation.

Working through those problems creates intuition that is difficult to get any other way. It changes the kinds of questions you notice and often exposes assumptions that would otherwise remain invisible.

But I also do not want to stop at “I built a cool robot.” Building something technically difficult is not automatically the same as producing a strong research contribution. The more interesting question is: What can I learn, demonstrate, or discover because I built this robot?

The robot should not only be the result. Sometimes it should be the instrument that makes a new question possible.

5. Learn how to compress engineering into a research contribution.

A robotics project can contain hundreds of difficult decisions: mechanical redesigns, calibration, controller tuning, data pipelines, sensor failures, software architecture, manufacturing problems, and long debugging sessions caused by something absurdly small.

All of that work can be genuinely difficult, but a paper cannot simply say, Trust me, this was extremely hard.

Research requires compression. Out of a huge amount of engineering effort, I need to identify a small number of precise contributions. What is actually new? Why does it matter? What evidence would convince someone that it works? What part of the result is generalizable beyond this particular system?

A good researcher needs to be able to turn a large and messy project into a clear intellectual argument. That is not just writing skill. It is part of the research itself.

6. Eventually, I want to help define the game, not only play it.

There is one kind of research that improves performance on an existing benchmark. There is another kind that changes what people decide to measure in the first place.

A new benchmark, a better evaluation protocol, a new formulation of a problem, or a dataset that enables questions people could not previously ask can sometimes have more lasting impact than simply pushing an existing metric a little higher.

That kind of work is powerful because other researchers begin operating inside a framework you helped create. At some point, I would like to move from asking “How do I perform well under the existing rules?” to asking “Are these actually the right rules?”

That seems like one of the points where a researcher starts influencing a field rather than simply participating in it.

7. A good environment matters, but I need to own something inside it.

Prestigious labs, excellent supervisors, strong collaborators, compute, hardware, funding, and institutional reputation all matter. A good environment can dramatically change what is possible.

But I do not want my research identity to be borrowed entirely from the environment around me. At the end of a project, I should be able to identify what I genuinely owned: the question I pushed, the idea I contributed, the system I understood deeply, the experiment I designed, or the direction I helped shape.

A strong environment should amplify ownership, not replace it.

This also seems like an important distinction when thinking about career growth. Being surrounded by impressive work is useful, but eventually I need to become someone who can generate that work rather than simply contribute to someone else's momentum.

8. Explore widely, then notice what keeps pulling me back.

I do not think early specialization is automatically good. Especially in robotics, wandering through different fields can be extremely productive because the field itself sits at the intersection of so many disciplines.

But permanent wandering is different from exploration. Eventually, patterns should start appearing. Maybe I repeatedly become interested in how robots learn from humans. Maybe I keep returning to embodiment, adaptation, uncertainty, interaction, control, representation, or autonomy.

A research direction may not arrive as a dramatic revelation. It may simply be the thing I keep finding myself curious about.

I want to pay attention to those repetitions instead of forcing myself to choose an identity too early. The goal is not to pick a label. It is to notice which questions continue to survive after the novelty of a new topic wears off.

9. Momentum matters more than one lucky paper.

One excellent result is impressive. What is even more convincing is when good work keeps happening.

A strong researcher eventually creates a particular expectation: whatever they work on next will probably be interesting too. That expectation cannot be built from one publication. It comes from repeatedly identifying worthwhile problems and producing convincing answers.

So the goal is not merely to produce one impressive project. It is to develop a process that can produce good research again and again.

That means learning how to choose problems, how to scope them, how to recognize dead ends, how to collaborate well, how to design convincing experiments, and how to turn failures into the next useful question.

10. The thing I probably need most is research taste.

This is the least measurable item on the list and maybe the most important.

Research taste is knowing which problem is worth months of work, which result is genuinely surprising, which complication matters and which is noise, when a method is elegant and when it is merely complicated, when to keep digging, when to abandon an idea, what to measure, and what is technically impressive but scientifically uninteresting.

I do not think this is something I can learn from a single class. It probably develops by reading a lot of research, building things, failing, discussing ideas with people, comparing different approaches, and repeatedly asking why I find certain work compelling.

If I had to condense all of this into the kind of researcher I would like to become, it would be someone who can move comfortably between ideas and actual systems without being defined by only one side of that divide.

Not someone who only builds models, and not someone who only builds robots. Someone who can recognize a worthwhile problem, understand it well enough to know which tools are actually needed, build or learn whatever is necessary to investigate it, and turn the result into something clear enough that other people can learn from, use, or build on it.

I want enough breadth to move across different parts of robotics without collecting skills just for the sake of collecting them, and enough depth to develop questions that are recognizably mine. I want to be able to make things work in the real world, but also know when a technically difficult system contains an interesting research question and when it is simply a difficult engineering project. I want individual projects to eventually connect into a direction rather than remain a pile of unrelated things I happened to find interesting.

And, more than any particular tool or topic, I want to develop the judgment to know what is worth working on, what is actually new, what evidence matters, and when a question is interesting enough to keep following.

That is probably the part I will have to keep learning for the longest.
